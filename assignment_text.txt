AI Team Intern Assignment —
The Audit
Timebox: designed for ~10 focused hours over 5 days. We grade
thinking-per-hour, not volume. A sharp 6-hour submission beats a
bloated 20-hour one.
Format: take-home + a 30-minute live defense session.
Ground rules (read these twice)
AI tools are allowed and expected. Claude, ChatGPT, Copilot, Cursor
— use whatever you want. We use them daily; pretending you won't
would be silly. This assignment is designed so that AI without your own
understanding produces a mediocre submission. Two rules make that
work:
1. The evidence rule. Every claim you make — "this line is a bug",
"this metric is misleading", "this conﬁg is faster" — must ship with
a minimal experiment demonstrating it: the exact command, the
before/after numbers, and one sentence on why the delta proves
your claim. A claimed ﬂaw without measured evidence scores
negative points. Conﬁdent hallucination is the one thing we
punish harder than being wrong.
2. The defense. In the live session you will re-derive your numbers,
modify your code on the spot, and answer counterfactuals ("if we
changed X, what happens to your conclusion?"). Anything in your
submission is fair game. If you can't defend it, don't submit it.
You must also submit AI_USAGE.md: a short honest summary of where
AI helped you and where it misled you. This is not a trap — "Claude
wrote 80% of my plotting code and conﬁdently invented a bug that didn't
exist" is a perfectly good entry. What we're checking is whether you
know which parts of your submission you understand.
The scenario
A previous intern benchmarked our tokenizer and our serving stack,
wrote REPORT_v0.md, and left. Leadership is about to make routing and
capacity decisions based on it. Your job is to audit the report before that
happens.
You are given (in starter_kit/):
•  fertility.py — the script that produced the tokenizer numbers
•  REPORT_v0.md — the report and its recommendations
•  corpus_sample/ — the tiny corpora the intern used (English +
Hindi, parallel line-by-line). These are smoke-test toys, not an
eval set.
•  bench/model_spec.md + bench/bench_log.csv — serving
setup and the load-test log behind Section 2 of the report
Part A — The tokenizer audit (50 pts)
A1. Build a real eval corpus (10 pts). The sample corpora are ~10
sentences. Assemble a proper multilingual eval set: at least 4 languages
including English, Hindi, and two Dravidian languages (e.g. Kannada,
Tamil, Telugu, Malayalam). Parallel corpora such as FLORES-200 are a
good starting point but the choice is yours — document it. State corpus
size, domain, any preprocessing, and one paragraph on what your
corpus cannot tell you (sample-size and domain caveats are signal, not
weakness).
A2. Audit the script and the metric (20 pts). fertility.py has
problems. Some are code bugs. At least one is a conceptual problem:
the code computes exactly what it says, but what it says is the wrong
thing to compute. And at least one thing in the script looks suspicious
but is actually ﬁne. For every ﬂaw you claim, apply the evidence rule:
isolate it, measure its eﬀect on the reported numbers, and state the
direction and magnitude of the distortion. Flagging the harmless thing as
a bug — without evidence — costs you points.
A3. Corrected analysis (12 pts). Recompute the cross-language
comparison properly, on your corpus from A1, with at least two
tokenizers (one of them multilingual/Indic-aware; open ones are ﬁne)
and at least two denominators (e.g. per whitespace word, per
grapheme cluster, per UTF-8 byte, per parallel sentence). Then answer
the question that actually matters: which single number should drive a
routing-and-cost decision, and why? Hint: think hard about what the
denominator is supposed to hold constant across languages.
A4. Recommendation memo (8 pts). ≤1 page. Your corrected headline
numbers, your routing recommendation, the biggest caveat, and the one
metric you would monitor in production to catch this analysis being
wrong.
Part B — Capacity reconciliation (20 pts)
Using bench/model_spec.md and bench/bench_log.csv:
B1 (7 pts). From the model spec alone, compute: (a) KV-cache bytes
per token, exactly; (b) the approximate maximum number of concurrent
4096-token sequences this GPU can hold. Show the arithmetic. Then
check your prediction against the log.
B2 (6 pts). The long-context sweep (prompt 3584) contains a throughput
anomaly relative to naive "throughput scales with batch" expectations.
Identify it, explain the mechanism using speciﬁc rows and columns, and
propose one conﬁg or deployment change with a predicted quantitative
eﬀect.
B3 (4 pts). REPORT_v0 Section 2 concludes that longer prompts give
better throughput and that batch 48 will deliver ~3200 tok/s. Both
conclusions come from the same misreading of one column. What is it,
what is the honest "goodput" of the batch-24 long-prompt row (derive it
from the log — there are two independent ways), and what should the
report have said?
B4 (3 pts). One paragraph: which single counter or metric from the
serving stack would you pull to conﬁrm your B2 mechanism, and what
value do you expect it to show?
Part C — Decision memo (15 pts)
Scenario: the product team wants our assistant's replies to sound casual
and conversational in Hindi, Kannada, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali and
Marathi — current outputs are too formal/textbook. Three paths are on
the table:
•  (a) an SFT pass on synthetic "casualized" response pairs
•  (b) a small (≤1B) inference-time rewriter model after the main
model
•  (c) prompt-engineering only
Constraints: one A100-80GB for 2 weeks, one native-speaker reviewer
(Hindi + Kannada only) for 10 h/week, launch review in 3 weeks, no
external API budget.
Write a ≤1-page recommendation containing, explicitly labelled: your
assumptions, back-of-envelope arithmetic (data volume, training or
serving cost, reviewer throughput), a success metric with a numeric
threshold, a kill criterion (the observation that would make you
abandon your chosen path, and by when), and the ﬁrst experiment you
would run on day 1. There is no single right answer; there are many
wrong ways to reason.
Deliverables
A repo (zip or git link) containing:
your-submission/
  NOTEBOOK.md        # chronological lab notebook (see be
  AI_USAGE.md
  partA/             # code, corpus prep scripts, correct
  partB/             # calculations + written answers
  partC/memo.md
NOTEBOOK.md is graded, not decorative. It is a chronological log of
hypothesis → experiment → result → revision, including dead ends. A
notebook with no dead ends and no surprises is, in our experience, a
notebook written after the fact.
Defense session (15 pts, and a gate)
30 minutes, screen shared, your repo open. Expect: "re-derive this
number", "run your script with this input I'm about to paste", "add this ﬂag
live", and counterfactuals. Fabricated evidence discovered at any point
— in the repo or in the defense — is an automatic fail regardless of
score.
